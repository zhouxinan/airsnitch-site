OverviewClient isolation is not what it claims to be

Client isolation (also known as AP isolation) is widely deployed to stop malicious Wi-Fi clients from attacking other clients on the same network. AirSnitch shows that this isolation is not standardized, and its guarantees vary substantially across vendors — leading to inconsistent, and often incomplete, enforcement in deployed networks.

We perform a structured analysis across the three boundaries where isolation is expected to hold:

A — Wi-Fi Encryption
Over-the-air confidentiality between clients, enforced by per-client and group keys.
B — IP / Routing
Layer-3 rules that should prevent clients from reaching each other through the gateway.
C — Link-Layer Switching
MAC-to-port learning across an AP's virtual Wi-Fi "ports" (BSSIDs).

The paper identifies several root causes for why isolation breaks in practice: shared Wi-Fi keys for group/broadcast traffic can be abused by insiders; isolation is frequently enforced at only one layer (MAC or IP) instead of consistently across all three; and client identity — MAC address, IP address, and key state — is weakly synchronized across the network stack, which enables bypasses between layers.

Attack TechniquesThree ways isolation fails

1. Shared-Key (GTK/IGTK) Abuse

Group and broadcast traffic on a Wi-Fi network is protected by a shared Group Temporal Key (GTK) and, for management frames, a shared Integrity GTK (IGTK). Because every client on a BSSID holds the same key, an insider can leverage it to craft and inject traffic that reaches other clients — a weakness that holds even under WPA2/3-Enterprise.

2. Gateway Bouncing

Even when direct layer-2 forwarding between clients is blocked, packets can be "bounced" off the default gateway when IP-layer isolation is missing or incomplete. The gateway itself becomes an unwitting relay between clients that are supposed to be isolated.

3. Port-Stealing Interception

By manipulating MAC-to-port learning across an AP's virtual Wi-Fi "ports" (its BSSIDs), an attacker can redirect traffic intended for a victim toward themselves instead — a switching-style port-stealing attack applied to the Wi-Fi link layer, enabling interception even where encryption and IP-layer isolation both hold.

PaperNDSS 2026 · San Diego, CA, USA · 23–27 February 2026

Abstract. To prevent malicious Wi-Fi clients from attacking other clients on the same network, vendors have introduced client isolation, a combination of mechanisms that block direct communication between clients. However, client isolation is not a standardized feature, making its security guarantees unclear.

In this paper, we undertake a structured security analysis of Wi-Fi client isolation and uncover new classes of attacks that bypass this protection. We identify several root causes behind these weaknesses. First, Wi-Fi keys that protect broadcast frames are improperly managed and can be abused to bypass client isolation. Second, isolation is often enforced at only the MAC or IP layer, but not both. Third, weak synchronization of a client's identity across the network stack allows one to bypass Wi-Fi client isolation at the network layer instead, enabling the interception of uplink and downlink traffic of other clients as well as internal backend devices. Every tested router and network was vulnerable to at least one attack. More broadly, the lack of standardization leads to inconsistent, ad hoc, and often incomplete implementations of isolation across vendors.

Building on these insights, we design and evaluate end-to-end attacks that enable full man-in-the-middle capabilities in modern Wi-Fi networks. Although client isolation effectively mitigates legacy attacks like ARP spoofing — long considered the only universal method for achieving machine-in-the-middle positioning in local area networks — our attack introduces a general and practical alternative that restores this capability, even in the presence of client isolation.

Contributions include:

  • Novel MitM primitives that break client isolation.
  • A demonstration that Wi-Fi client isolation in single-BSSID networks (e.g., home networks) is fundamentally broken for all WPA versions.
  • Bypasses extended to multi-AP and multi-SSID enterprise-style deployments.
  • A demonstration that these attacks can intercept traffic of internal wired devices, enabling higher-layer attacks such as breaking weak back-end RADIUS passwords.

The attacks were evaluated against five recent home routers from popular vendors, two widely-used open-source router distributions, a locally configured enterprise testbed, and two university networks in the wild.

FAQQuestions & Answers

Last updated 3/5/2026

What are the key discoveries of this paper?

This work reminds practitioners of something easy to forget: Wi-Fi is essentially Ethernet extended into the wireless domain, and it inherits some of Ethernet's structural insecurities. To support mobility and seamless roaming, modern deployments allow clients to move across multiple BSSIDs on the same AP — for example, transparently shifting from 2.4 GHz to 5 GHz under load. Each BSSID effectively behaves like a hardware or virtualized layer-1 port on the AP.

As a result, an attacker can abuse layer-1 port states in a target Wi-Fi infrastructure to steal traffic and perform MitM attacks. This stems from vulnerable protocol-infrastructure interactions in Wi-Fi: its over-the-air encryption protocols (WEP, TKIP, CCMP, GCMP) were not designed to cooperate with layer-2 switching. An attacker can disrupt the layer-2 switching tables (MAC-to-port mappings) in an AP to bypass Wi-Fi encryption at the infrastructure level.

In large enterprise environments with dozens or hundreds of APs and BSSIDs, this creates a broad and often under-examined attack surface. An attacker who can manipulate layer-1 port states — for example, from a guest SSID — may be able to interfere with or disrupt other SSIDs even when they are protected by WPA2/3-Enterprise. There can also be weak layer-3 isolation between guest and main networks that allows an attacker to inject packets. Finally, the Wi-Fi standard itself has design weaknesses, such as shared GTK/IGTK, that allow breaking client isolation even under WPA2/3-Enterprise.

What does "client isolation" aim to prevent?

Direct client-to-client interception, injection, and communication within the same Wi-Fi LAN — including across BSSIDs, APs, and SSIDs in a shared distribution system — when enforced correctly. Our research shows that, in the real world, such isolation is frequently problematic and can be bypassed by attackers operating at different networking layers.

What should defenders take away?

Isolation needs a clear, standardized definition and must be enforced consistently across encryption, routing, and switching. If any one boundary is weak — shared group keys, missing IP-layer rules, or leaky switching behavior — isolation can fail in practice. Do not assume that WPA3-Enterprise alone provides sufficient security for an enterprise network; strengthen the networking infrastructure comprehensively instead.

I'm a home/SOHO Wi-Fi user. What's the impact of this research on me?

For WPA2-Personal networks: if you share a passphrase with other users, those users can decrypt your traffic over the air by deriving your Wi-Fi PTK keys. For WPA3-Personal networks: if you share a passphrase with other users, they can host a fake AP with the same passphrase and induce your client to connect to that rogue AP, exposing your traffic. Many Wi-Fi routers offer a "guest network" feature; one major finding of the paper is that such guest networks can unintentionally become an attack surface, allowing an attacker to break the security of the main network and steal its traffic.

How should enterprise Wi-Fi defenses evolve in response to AirSnitch?

A non-exhaustive checklist:

(1) Do not assume WPA2/3-Enterprise alone provides full security — strengthen network isolation across the infrastructure.
(2) Make sure your guest SSID does not become an attack surface against the main network: every guest BSSID should be isolated from other BSSIDs on the same AP.
(3) Disable shared GTK/IGTK on BSSIDs where possible to stop over-the-air GTK/IGTK abuse.
(4) Segment your wired network so attackers cannot perform cross-AP attacks.
(5) Keep operating systems updated to their latest versions.
(6) Use firewalls to block "server-triggered port restoration," as described in the paper.
(7) Use a VPN even for internal network traffic.

Are you using ARP spoofing to perform MitM attacks?

No. Some attacks (e.g., Port Stealing) work at a layer below ARP. Some (e.g., Gateway Bouncing) work at layer 3 (IP). Some (e.g., GTK/IGTK Abuse) abuse layer-2 Wi-Fi encryption. All of our attack techniques, however, incorporate layer-1 port state reasoning.

Why are your attacks characterized as Wi-Fi–specific, given that port stealing is a general layer-2 technique?

Before this research, it was unknown whether port stealing could bypass Wi-Fi encryption. One of our findings is that Wi-Fi cryptography can be bypassed with port stealing because there is no authentication at this even-lower networking layer. By identifying this gap in layer-1 port state reasoning, we develop attack primitives that work against all existing Wi-Fi encryption protocols. GTK/IGTK Abuse is likewise a Wi-Fi–specific technique.

Does using VLANs block all of these attacks?

No. VLANs can leak in some cases (see "VLAN hopping" for background), and Gateway Bouncing also bypasses client isolation at the IP layer. Even with VLAN segmentation separating a guest attacker from the main WPA2/3-Enterprise SSID on one AP, an attacker may still abuse other APs to intercept WPA2/3-Enterprise traffic — see Cross-AP MitM Attacks in the paper.

Why do you say AirSnitch "might have the potential to enable advanced cyberattacks"?

A few concrete examples:

(1) DNS cache poisoning: an attacker can use our bidirectional MitM primitive to directly learn the DNS transaction ID and UDP port number, making cache poisoning substantially easier.
(2) DHCP poisoning: the DHCP transaction ID (8 bytes) used to be very difficult to guess or brute-force; our bidirectional MitM primitive lets an attacker obtain it directly.
(3) TCP session hijacking: an attacker can use our bidirectional MitM primitive to learn TCP sequence numbers even under strong randomization.
(4) ARP poisoning: our bidirectional MitM primitive can revive ARP poisoning, which client isolation used to block; because our bypass works at a layer below ARP, it can deliver ARP-poisoning frames even under client isolation.
(5) ICMP redirects: our bidirectional MitM primitive can revive ICMP redirect attacks. ICMP redirects rely on an attacker knowing a previous packet's header fields — specifically the source/destination IP tuple and part of the original IP header that must be quoted in the redirect message — so that a forged redirect appears consistent with an in-path router that legitimately observed and forwarded the triggering packet. An attacker using our MitM primitive can obtain these header fields.

Why do you say AirSnitch "bypasses Wi-Fi encryption"?

Although we did not cryptanalyze AES-CCMP or AES-GCMP, we still bypass Wi-Fi encryption by abusing low-level layer-1 port states. As a result, victims continue to see a "Wi-Fi padlock" and retain connectivity, while their traffic is stolen through other BSSIDs or APs. AirSnitch is an active attack that achieves this outcome.

Isn't the attack weak, since it requires a WPA2/3 passphrase?

Not always. An attacker can launch the attack from an open SSID — for example, a guest network — and pivot into a passphrase-protected main network if client/network isolation between the two is implemented incorrectly.

Does switching to WPA3 keep me safe?

Not always. First, WPA3 remains vulnerable to the GTK/IGTK Abuse attacks described in the paper. Second, if an AP hosts a WPA3 BSSID alongside an open BSSID, and network/client isolation between them is implemented incorrectly, an attacker can launch attacks from the open BSSID to perform a MitM against the WPA3 BSSID.
